PATZ1 · nucleosome by nucleosome

An explorable guide to our 2026 paper in Communications Biology

How PATZ1 clears nucleosomes off the genes injured neurons need to regrow

When a spinal cord injury cuts the axons of adult brain neurons, those axons almost never grow back. Part of the reason is how the neurons package their DNA. The switches that control their growth genes stay wrapped up in nucleosomes, where most transcription factors can't get at them. In this paper we show that a transcription factor called PATZ1 pushes many of those nucleosomes off.

  • With PATZ1PATZ1 lowers median nucleosome occupancy by 86%, and the nucleosomes that remain sit in more consistent positions.
  • At enhancersMost of the change happens at enhancers. PATZ1 binds them, and the number of active (H3K27ac-marked) enhancer regions more than triples.

Background

Why adult neurons don't regrow

Corticospinal neurons live in the motor cortex and send their axons all the way down the spinal cord. A spinal cord injury cuts those axons. Peripheral neurons can regrow a cut axon at any age, and so can young cortical neurons, but adult corticospinal neurons mostly can't.

Some of the blame goes to the scar that forms in the injured cord. A lot of it, though, sits inside the neuron. As cortical neurons mature, they gradually close off the regulatory DNA around their growth genes. Once that happens, the transcription factors that could turn those genes back on can't reach them.

In our earlier work, we found that giving injured corticospinal neurons extra PATZ1 reopened many growth-related genes and enhancers. That study measured accessibility, which tells you whether a piece of DNA is open or closed. It couldn't tell us what was happening to the packaging itself, so in this paper we looked at it one nucleosome at a time.

(POZ/BTB and AT-hook containing zinc finger 1) belongs to the POK family of transcription factors. It grips DNA with zinc fingers and an AT-hook at one end. At the other end, its BTB domain pulls in co-repressors and chromatin-modifying complexes.

It has also been described as a "stripe" transcription factor, meaning it binds GC-rich DNA all over the genome, helps open it up, and helps partner factors bind (Zhao et al., Mol. Cell 2022). That made it a good candidate for physically rearranging chromatin.

Our earlier study is Menon et al., bioRxiv 2025. The single-nucleus ATAC-seq data we reanalyse here come from that work.

Primer

Two numbers that describe a nucleosome

DNA wraps around little spools of histone proteins, about 147 base pairs per spool. Each wrapped spool is a nucleosome. Most transcription factors can't bind DNA that's wrapped up like this.

We can't watch one nucleosome in one cell. What we can measure is a whole population of cells, and for each nucleosome position we get two numbers. Occupancy is the fraction of cells that have a nucleosome at that spot. Fuzziness is how much its exact position shifts from cell to cell. You can play with both below.

Finding 1

Injury alone leaves nucleosomes where they were

First we needed a baseline. One week after injury, nucleosomes in L5 ET neurons looked almost exactly like they did in uninjured mice. Median occupancy went from 0.105 to 0.100, and median fuzziness went from 23.55 to 22.81 bp.

So the injured neuron isn't clearing its growth-gene packaging on its own, which fits with how poorly these neurons regrow. It also gives us a clean comparison. Whatever changes once we add PATZ1, we know injury alone wouldn't have done it.

Finding 2

PATZ1 removes nucleosomes and sharpens the ones that stay

Adding PATZ1 to injured neurons made a big difference. Median occupancy dropped from 0.100 to 0.013, an 86% fall. NucleoATAC also found far fewer nucleosomes overall, 33,448 compared with 123,950 in injured neurons.

The nucleosomes that were left also sat in more consistent positions, with median fuzziness going from 22.81 to 17.63 bp. This second result helps explain what PATZ1 is doing. If it were just making chromatin generally unstable, the leftover nucleosomes should have become fuzzier, and they became sharper instead. That looks like active reorganization.

Finding 3

Where the nucleosomes go missing

A genome-wide median can hide where things are actually changing, so we zoomed in on two sets of regions. The first was the set of open regions whose ATAC signal changed with PATZ1 (differentially accessible regions). The second was the set of genes whose RNA levels changed with PATZ1 (differentially expressed genes). For each region we asked what had happened to its nucleosome.

Most of the regions PATZ1 opened had already lost their nucleosomes in injured neurons, and PATZ1 opened them up further. A smaller group, about 11% of regions and 20% of responsive genes, still had a nucleosome that PATZ1 then removed. Even where a nucleosome stayed, it nearly always got sparser and more sharply positioned.

We then used Gene Ontology pathways to ask which kinds of genes were losing nucleosomes.

Finding 4

PATZ1 opens up enhancers

Enhancers switch genes on, often from quite far away, and active ones carry a histone mark called H3K27ac. We mapped H3K27ac with CUT&RUN in the motor cortex of injured and PATZ1 mice. Then we checked what the nucleosomes at those enhancers were doing.

About three-quarters of the enhancers with nucleosome calls (76.1%) were PATZ1-specific. So most of what PATZ1 does here is open enhancers that were closed in injured neurons, with a smaller effect on enhancers that were already active.

Finding 5

Checking the result with a histone antibody

Everything up to this point relies on working out nucleosome positions from DNA fragment sizes. To check it a different way, we ran CUT&RUN with an antibody against histone H3, a protein found in every nucleosome.

Finding 6

PATZ1 sits right on active enhancers

Next we wanted to know whether PATZ1 is physically sitting at these enhancers. CUT&RUN against PATZ1 found 5,764 binding peaks in the injured motor cortex, and we lined these up against the 3,708 H3K27ac peaks.

Finding 7

Does PATZ1 need acetylation?

H3K27ac is added by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) like p300 and CBP, and earlier studies have tied these enzymes to axon regeneration. So we wondered whether PATZ1 needs them to keep chromatin open.

We tested this in N2A cells, where we can block HAT activity with a drug called A-485. In these cells PATZ1 opened 9,887 regions that were closed in control cells. Then we added the inhibitor to see which of them stayed open.

Stayed open without acetylation

  • Axon guidance
  • Nervous system development
  • System development
  • Intracellular signalling, signal transduction
  • Protein autophosphorylation
  • RNA polymerase II activation, transcription activation

Closed when acetylation was blocked

  • Cell differentiation
  • Negative regulation of proliferation
  • Heart and muscle organ development
  • Reactive oxygen species metabolism
  • SMAD signalling, small GTPase signalling
  • Regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II

The growth-related regions mostly stayed open without acetylation. So PATZ1 probably has another way of keeping them open, perhaps by bringing in a different remodelling complex, although we haven't tested that directly.

A caveat

N2A cells aren't corticospinal neurons, so we read these results with some care. In N2A cells PATZ1 changed accessibility at specific peaks, but it didn't cause the large-scale nucleosome loss we saw in injured neurons, and the inhibitor barely changed nucleosome profiles. The full effect seems to need something about neurons, maybe cofactors that N2A cells lack. PATZ1 is known to act differently in different cell types. In fibroblasts, for example, it actually slows down reprogramming.

  • Total peak counts were similar in all four conditions: DMSO 63,280; A-485 59,644; PATZ1 + DMSO 63,663; PATZ1 + A-485 60,188.
  • PATZ1 + DMSO vs DMSO: 53,489 shared peaks (75%), 9,887 only with PATZ1 (14%), and 7,591 only in controls (11%).
  • Accessibility near the TSS was weaker with PATZ1 in N2A cells, which is the opposite of what we see in injured corticospinal neurons.
  • Running NucleoATAC on the N2A data, occupancy and fuzziness barely changed with A-485, with or without PATZ1. Matched nucleosomes leaned slightly toward higher occupancy under A-485 (67.7% went up with GFP only, 55.7% with PATZ1), with no overall change in fuzziness.

Summary

Putting it together

  1. After a spinal cord injury, corticospinal neurons keep their nucleosomes more or less where they were, and the enhancers of growth genes stay packed.

    Finding 1

  2. PATZ1 binds strongly active enhancers far more often than you'd expect by chance.

    Finding 6

  3. At those regions nucleosomes get removed, and the ones left behind sit in more precise positions. Our H3 antibody data agree with this.

    Findings 2, 3 and 5

  4. The number of active enhancers more than triples, mostly near genes for axon guidance, neuron development and synapses. Some of this opening doesn't depend on acetylation.

    Findings 4 and 7

Our reading of this is that adult neurons haven't lost their growth programme for good. A lot of it is packed away under nucleosomes, and PATZ1 can clear some of that away.

Limitations

What we don't know yet

We only see open chromatinNucleoATAC only calls nucleosomes inside accessible regions. MNase-seq would also cover closed chromatin.
Opening DNA isn't the same as regrowing axonsWe haven't shown that PATZ1 binding at these sites makes axons regenerate. PATZ1 may get these regions ready, and another factor may be needed to actually use them.
Do the nucleosomes come back?We looked one week after injury. We don't yet know whether these regions stay open long enough to help in chronic injury.
How the pieces fit togetherNucleosome removal, histone marks and 3D looping (such as cohesin-driven promoter-enhancer contacts) all seem to matter, but we don't know which comes first.
